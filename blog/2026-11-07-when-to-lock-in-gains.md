# Up 100% and Afraid of Getting Greedy? How to Decide When to Lock In Gains

A position that has doubled is a good problem to have, and it's still stressful. Sell now and it might double again without you. Hold and it might give half of it back. Semiconductor funds in particular can move 20% or 30% in a few weeks in either direction, so the question feels urgent.

I can't tell you what to do with your position. I don't know your finances, your taxes or your goals, and anyone in a comment section who says "sell half" doesn't either. What I can do is share the ways experienced investors and traders approach this decision, so you make it on purpose instead of in a panic.

<!--truncate-->

## First, separate the two questions

"When do I lock in gains?" is really two questions:

1. **How much of my total money should be in this one position?**
2. **What would make me sell, and is it written down?**

Most of the stress comes from trying to answer both at once, live, while watching the price.

## Question 1: How big is it now?

Start with arithmetic, not prediction. If the fund was 10% of your portfolio and doubled while everything else stayed flat, it's now about 18%. You never decided to put 18% of your money into one sector. The market did.

Ask yourself:

- **If I had this amount in cash today, would I put all of it into this fund?** If the answer is no, the position is bigger than you'd choose today.
- **What would a 40% drop do to me?** Sector funds have fallen that much before. Picture the dollar amount, not the percentage.
- **What else am I exposed to?** If you work in tech, hold tech stocks in your retirement plan, and own a semiconductor fund, those can all fall together.
- **Is it a leveraged fund?** Two-times and three-times funds reset every day and behave very differently from the underlying index over longer periods, especially when prices swing. If yours is one, read its prospectus before deciding anything else.

## Question 2: Five ways people decide when to sell

None of these is "right." Each one trades some upside for some peace of mind.

**1. Trim back to a target size (rebalancing).**
Decide what share of your portfolio this position should be, then sell only the part above it. You keep exposure to further gains and stop the position from growing into a risk you never chose. Many investors do this on a schedule, for example once a quarter or once a year.

**2. Take your original investment off the table.**
Sell enough to recover what you put in, and let the rest ride. It doesn't change the math much, but many people find it removes the emotional pressure, because the rest feels like "house money." (It isn't, and it can still be lost. It just feels that way.)

**3. Sell in planned steps.**
For example, sell a set slice at preset price levels or on preset dates. You'll never sell at the exact top, but you'll never be all-in at the top either.

**4. Use a trailing stop.**
A trailing stop follows the price up and sells if the price falls a set percentage from its high. Two things to know before using one:
- In a fast drop, or when the market opens sharply lower, a stop becomes a market order and can fill well below your stop price.
- Set it too tight on a volatile fund and normal swings will knock you out. Set it too loose and it protects less than you think.

**5. Decide by your plan, not the price.**
If this money has a job (a house down payment in two years, tuition, retirement in five years), the timeline may matter more than the chart. Money you need soon has less room to recover from a drop.

## Don't forget taxes

Selling in a taxable account can create a tax bill, and the rules depend on where you live, how long you've held the position and what type of account it's in. In the US, for example, gains on positions held longer than a year are usually taxed at a lower rate than shorter-term gains, and sales inside retirement accounts like an IRA follow different rules. Sometimes waiting a few weeks changes the bill a lot. A quick conversation with a tax professional before a big sale is usually worth it.

## The real problem is deciding under pressure

I started trading in 2016, and the lesson that stuck the most is that the hardest part of trading is the person, not the market. When a position is up 100%, greed says "it'll keep going." When it drops 15% from the high, fear says "get out now." Both feelings are loudest exactly when you most need a clear head.

That's why I think the most useful step isn't picking the perfect method. It's **writing your rule down before the next big move**, while you're calm:

> "I'll keep this position at no more than __% of my portfolio. I'll trim back to that level every __. If it falls __% from its high, I'll __."

Then follow it, and only change it on a calm day, never in the middle of a big move.

That idea, rules decided in advance and followed the same way every time, is what I built Atomik around for futures traders. For a fund at your broker, you don't need any special software. The rule and the order types you already have are enough.

## The short version

- Look at what share of your portfolio the position is now, not just the percent gain.
- Pick a method that fits your goals: trim to a target size, take your original money out, sell in steps, use a trailing stop, or go by your timeline.
- Check the tax impact before you sell.
- Write the rule down and follow it.

Missing some upside isn't the same as losing money. Whatever you decide, decide it on purpose.
